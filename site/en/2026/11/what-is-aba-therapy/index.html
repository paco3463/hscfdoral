What Is ABA Therapy? A Clear Guide for Parents

A plain-language guide to ABA therapy for children with autism: what it is, the different styles, what research shows and doesn't, the concerns of autistic adults, and the signs of a good, family-centred program.

What is ABA therapy? In short, it is a treatment that uses the science of learning to teach useful skills, like communicating, playing or getting dressed, and to reduce behaviors that get in the way of daily life2, 3. It is one of the most common supports for children with autism, and most evidence-based autism treatments are built on its principles1. Research suggests it can help, but the quality of the studies is still limited, and a good program should fit your child and your family1, 4, 5.

What is ABA therapy?

ABA stands for Applied Behavior Analysis. It is a science that uses learning principles to improve people's lives3. It looks at how the environment affects behavior, assesses before it intervenes, and makes decisions based on data3.

In practice, ABA encourages behaviors that help a child and discourages behaviors that get in the way, and it tracks progress closely2. A "behavior" is not just being good or bad. Asking for water with words or a gesture, waiting for a turn or washing hands are also behaviors that can be taught.

ABA is not a single technique. There are very different styles:

  • Discrete trial training: the adult gives step-by-step instructions to teach a specific skill and rewards the desired response2.
  • Naturalistic approaches: teaching happens during play and daily routines, following the child's interests. One example is pivotal response training (PRT), which takes place in a natural setting rather than a clinic1, 2.
  • Naturalistic developmental behavioral interventions (NDBIs): these blend ABA with what we know about child development. They take place in natural settings, the child and adult share control of the activity, and they use natural rewards6. The Early Start Denver Model, for children 12 to 48 months old, is one of them; parents and therapists use play to teach2.

In the United States, the Behavior Analyst Certification Board (BACB) certifies ABA practitioners at three levels: Registered Behavior Technician (RBT), Board Certified Assistant Behavior Analyst (BCaBA) and Board Certified Behavior Analyst (BCBA)3. ABA programs are usually designed and supervised by a professional certified in behavior analysis1.

Signs of a good ABA program

Not all ABA programs are the same. These are signs a program is on the right track:

  • It starts with an assessment of your child's strengths and needs1, 3.
  • Goals are chosen with you, and with your child when possible. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) says the clinician works with the family and the child to decide which skills to target1.
  • It is individual. Treatment is adjusted to each child's age, strengths and challenges1.
  • It includes you. The AAP says comprehensive programs should include parents, and families should take part in every decision1.
  • It tracks progress with data and changes the plan when something isn't working1, 3.
  • It relies mostly on positive reinforcement, which means rewarding and motivating rather than punishing7.
  • Your child seems comfortable with the therapists most of the time.

Tip: ask to watch a session, and ask "why does this goal matter for my child?" A good team will be glad to explain.

What research says about ABA therapy

The AAP notes that most evidence-based autism treatments are based on ABA principles1. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) say behavioral approaches have the most evidence for treating symptoms of autism2.

Still, the quality of that evidence matters. A Cochrane review looked at early intensive behavioral intervention (EIBI), a form of ABA with many hours a week for young children4. It found only 5 studies, and just one was a randomized trial4. Children improved in adaptive behavior, meaning everyday living skills, but the quality of the evidence was low. For autism symptom severity, it was very low4.

A larger 2023 analysis pulled together 252 studies of interventions for young children with autism5. It found that several types of intervention have positive effects, but in different areas. Behavioral interventions improved social-emotional skills or challenging behavior. Naturalistic interventions (NDBIs) were linked to gains in language, play, social communication and adaptive behavior5. The authors also warn that many studies are low quality and that possible harms were rarely monitored well5.

Concerns from autistic adults

Some autistic adults and neurodiversity advocates have raised serious concerns about ABA7. In one small study, 10 autistic adults looked back on their childhood ABA, and most described it as harmful8. Because the study is so small, it can't support broad conclusions, but it shares experiences worth hearing8.

The most common concerns are7:

  • The use of punishment in older programs, including physical punishment.
  • Rigid programs with about 40 hours a week for every child.
  • Trying to stop harmless behaviors, like hand-flapping or rocking.
  • Choosing goals without asking the autistic person.

Even authors who support ABA agree that some of these concerns are valid. They recommend favoring positive reinforcement, setting hours for each child, including the person or their family in choosing goals, and measuring quality of life, not just behavior7.

What you can do at home

  1. Notice what your child loves. Favorite games, songs and objects are powerful tools. Naturalistic approaches use those interests to teach2, 6.
  2. Think about your goals. Write down three things that would make life easier for your child and your family, like asking for help, sleeping better or a calm trip to the grocery store. Bring them to the first meeting.
  3. Prepare your questions. For example: Who designs and supervises the plan? How do you choose goals? How will I take part? What do you do if my child gets upset in a session? How do you measure progress?
  4. Practice in daily routines. Ask the team to show you how to support the same skills at mealtime, bath time or play. Programs should include parents1.
  5. Listen to your child. Notice how they feel before and after sessions. Their well-being is an important signal.
  6. Take care of yourself. Raising a child with special needs can be exhausting. Getting support for yourself helps your child too.

When to get help

Talk with your pediatrician or a specialist if:

  • Your child isn't talking, pointing or responding to their name like other children their age.
  • You are worried about their development, their play or how they relate to others.
  • Your child already has an autism diagnosis and isn't getting support yet.

You don't need to wait for a definite diagnosis to start services for a developmental delay1. If your child is younger than 3, they can get assessment and intervention through your state's early intervention program1.

If your child is already in ABA, talk with the team and consider a second opinion if:

  • You aren't allowed to watch sessions, or no one explains the plan.
  • The goals don't make sense for your child or your family.
  • Your child gets very upset before or during sessions, again and again.
  • Months go by and the data show no progress.

To read more, see the CDC page on autism treatment.

Frequently asked questions

What is ABA therapy in simple words?

ABA (Applied Behavior Analysis) uses the science of learning to teach useful skills, like communication, play and self-care, and to reduce behaviors that get in the way of daily life. It assesses first, sets specific goals and tracks progress with data. Most evidence-based autism treatments are based on its principles.

Does ABA therapy work?

Research suggests ABA-based programs can help children with autism build skills, and the CDC says behavioral approaches have the most evidence. But reviews rate much of the evidence as low quality, and possible harms have rarely been measured well. Results vary from child to child, so progress should be tracked and the plan adjusted.

Why do some autistic adults criticize ABA?

Some autistic adults and advocates object to the punishment used in older programs, very rigid schedules of about 40 hours a week, efforts to stop harmless behaviors like hand-flapping, and goals chosen without their input. Even researchers who support ABA agree some of these concerns are valid and recommend positive reinforcement, individualized hours and goals chosen with the person and family.

Is ABA the same as naturalistic or play-based therapy?

Not exactly. Older ABA styles are very structured, with step-by-step instructions at a table. Naturalistic developmental behavioral interventions (NDBIs), like the Early Start Denver Model, use ABA strategies during play and daily routines, follow the child's interests and share control between the child and adult.

Do I need an autism diagnosis to start services?

According to the American Academy of Pediatrics, a definite diagnosis is not needed to start services for a documented developmental delay. Children under 3 can be assessed and get help through their state's early intervention program. Insurance coverage for ABA usually depends on your plan, so check with your insurer or provider.
