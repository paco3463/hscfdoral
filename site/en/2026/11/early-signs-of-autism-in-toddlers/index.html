Early Signs of Autism in Toddlers: A Guide by Age for Parents

A plain-language guide to the early signs of autism in toddlers by age, why one sign is not a diagnosis, the M-CHAT-R/F screening at 18 and 24 months, and the steps to take in Florida if you are worried, with or without a diagnosis.

The early signs of autism in toddlers usually show up before age 2 and mostly involve social communication and play: for example, not responding to their name by about 9 to 12 months, not pointing to show you something by about 14 to 18 months, or repeating the same actions with toys over and over1, 2, 4. One sign alone does not mean a child is autistic, and some autistic children don't show these signs at all2, 4. If something worries you, talk to your pediatrician and ask for an evaluation: you don't need a diagnosis to get early help1, 8.

What is autism?

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a difference in development that affects how a person communicates, relates to others, learns and behaves3. It is called a "spectrum" because autistic people have a wide range of characteristics, needs, strengths and challenges3. Many have strengths such as remembering details for a long time, learning well by seeing or hearing, or doing very well in music, art, math or science3.

Autism can be diagnosed at any age, but the first signs usually appear in the first two years of life3. A specialist can usually make a reliable diagnosis by age 2, and sometimes as early as 18 months1, 3. Knowing early means support can start sooner3.

Early signs of autism in toddlers, by age

Every child develops at their own pace. These ages come from the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Use them as a guide for talking with your pediatrician, not as a test1, 2.

  • By about 9 months: doesn't respond when you call their name, or shows few facial expressions such as happy, sad or surprised2.
  • By about 12 months: uses few or no gestures, like waving bye-bye, and doesn't play simple back-and-forth games like pat-a-cake1, 2.
  • By about 14 to 15 months: doesn't point at things to show interest, or bring you objects to show you1, 2.
  • By about 18 months: doesn't point to share something interesting, and doesn't pretend play, like feeding a doll1, 2.
  • By about 24 months: doesn't seem to notice when someone else is hurt or upset2.
  • By about 3 years: doesn't notice other children or join them in play2.

Social communication

In toddlers, researchers have found that the first differences are often in social attention: looking at faces less, smiling back less, imitating less, or sharing attention with you less, such as looking where you point4. Many parents say one of their first concerns was that their child didn't turn when they heard their name4. Some children also have delayed language1, 2.

Play, interests and repetitive behaviors

  • Lines up toys and gets upset when someone changes the order2.
  • Plays with toys the same way every time, or focuses on one part, like the wheels on a toy car2.
  • Gets very upset by small changes in routine1, 2.
  • Repeats words or phrases over and over; this is called echolalia1, 2.
  • Flaps hands, rocks or spins in circles1, 2.
  • Reacts in unusual ways to sounds, smells, tastes, textures or lights1, 2.

When a child loses skills

Some children develop as expected and then stop using words or social skills they already had. The AAP estimates this happens to about one in four autistic children, most often between 18 and 24 months1, 4. Losing skills at any age is a reason to talk with your pediatrician soon9.

Remember: many of these behaviors are also seen in children who are not autistic, and an autistic child may have none of the behaviors on the list2. No single sign fits every child4. What matters is the overall picture, and a professional evaluates that.

What research says about early signs of autism

A group of experts reviewed the research on early identification of autism4. They concluded that between 12 and 24 months, the most useful signs are less social attention and communication and more repetitive behavior with objects4. Reliable signs have not yet been found before 12 months, and some studies saw no differences at 6 months4. Autism also shows up in very different ways: some children show differences very early, others have a speech delay in their second year, and others lose skills after a period of typical development4.

The M-CHAT-R/F: the 18- and 24-month screening

The AAP recommends that every child have an autism-specific screening at the 18- and 24-month checkups, in addition to general developmental screening at 9, 18 and 30 months1, 8. One of the most widely used tools is the M-CHAT-R/F, a parent questionnaire for children 16 to 30 months old1. If the result shows some risk, the professional asks follow-up questions to clarify the answers1.

In the study that validated the M-CHAT-R/F, nearly half (47.5%) of the children who screened positive after follow-up were diagnosed with autism, and almost all (94.6%) had some developmental delay or concern5. In other words, a positive screen is not a diagnosis, but it is a clear sign that an evaluation is worthwhile1, 5. A systematic review of 50 studies found that the M-CHAT identifies most autistic children, and that the follow-up questions greatly reduce false positives6. Still, accuracy varied a lot between studies, some autistic children don't screen positive, and few studies repeated the screening at both 18 and 24 months6. So if you are still worried after a normal screen, tell your pediatrician.

Where experts disagree

In 2016, the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF) concluded that there is not enough evidence to know whether the benefits of screening all young children, when no one has concerns, outweigh the harms7. That is not a recommendation against screening. The Task Force also says that when parents or clinicians raise concerns, those concerns should be listened to carefully and checked with validated tools such as the M-CHAT7. The AAP continues to recommend screening at 18 and 24 months1.

What you can do at home

  1. Track milestones. The CDC has checklists by age and a free app, Milestone Tracker (in Spanish, Sigamos el Desarrollo)8, 9. Bring them to every checkup.
  2. Write down what you see. Note specific examples and when they started: "doesn't turn when I call him," "lines up his cars." That gives your pediatrician clear information.
  3. Play face to face. Get down to your child's level, follow what interests them and wait for their response. Name what they look at or point to.
  4. Ask about autism screening at the 18- and 24-month checkups, even if no one has mentioned it1, 8.
  5. Trust your instincts. Many parents notice something before 18 to 24 months4. If you are worried, don't wait to see if it "goes away"8.

When to seek help

Talk with your pediatrician soon if:

  • Your child doesn't respond to their name by about 12 months1.
  • Doesn't point to show you things by about 14 to 18 months1, 2.
  • Doesn't pretend play by about 18 months1.
  • Loses words or social skills they had, at any age9.
  • Screened positive on the M-CHAT-R/F or another screening tool1.
  • Isn't reaching one or more milestones for their age, or something just doesn't feel right to you8, 9.

Steps you can take

  1. Talk with your pediatrician and share your notes. Ask for a screening and, if needed, a referral to a specialist, such as a developmental pediatrician, child neurologist or child psychologist8.
  2. If your child is under 3, call your early intervention program. In Florida it is called Early Steps, and it serves children from birth to 36 months who show signs of developmental delay or whose parents or doctors have concerns10.
  3. If your child is 3 or older, call a local public elementary school, even if your child doesn't go there, and ask for a preschool special education evaluation8.

You don't need to wait for a diagnosis or a doctor's referral to request this evaluation, and it is free8. The AAP also says a definitive diagnosis is not needed to start services for a developmental delay, and that a child who screens positive should be referred for a diagnostic evaluation and intervention services at the same time1.

For more in plain language, see the CDC's page Concerned About Your Child's Development?.

Frequently asked questions

What are the first signs of autism in a toddler?

The earliest signs usually involve social communication and play: not responding to their name by about 12 months, not pointing to show you things by about 14 to 18 months, little pretend play by 18 months, and repeating the same actions with toys. Signs vary a lot, and many of these behaviors also appear in children who are not autistic. A professional looks at the overall picture.

At what age can autism be diagnosed?

Signs usually appear in the first two years of life, and a specialist can usually make a reliable diagnosis by age 2, sometimes as early as 18 months. Autism can also be diagnosed later, at any age.

What is the M-CHAT-R/F?

It is a short questionnaire for parents of children 16 to 30 months old, used to screen for autism at the 18- and 24-month checkups. If the result shows some risk, the clinician asks follow-up questions. A positive result is not a diagnosis, but most children who screen positive have some developmental difference that deserves an evaluation.

Do I need a diagnosis to get early intervention?

No. You can ask for a free evaluation without a diagnosis or a doctor's referral. In Florida, children under 3 are served by Early Steps, and for children 3 and older you can call a local public elementary school and ask for a preschool special education evaluation.

Does one sign mean my child is autistic?

No. No single sign fits every autistic child, and some autistic children show none of the common signs. If something worries you, write down what you see and talk to your pediatrician, who can screen your child and refer you to a specialist if needed.
