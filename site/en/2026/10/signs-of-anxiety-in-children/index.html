Signs of Anxiety in Children and How Parents Can Help

How to tell everyday worry from an anxiety disorder in children, which treatments research supports, and practical ways parents can help at home.

Common signs of anxiety in children include worry that does not go away, stomachaches or headaches, trouble sleeping, irritability, and avoiding places like school2. Some fear is a normal part of growing up, but when worry lasts, shows up in many situations and gets in the way of daily life, it may be an anxiety disorder3, 5. The good news is that childhood anxiety is very treatable, and parents can play a big part in helping6, 8.

What does anxiety in children look like?

Anxiety is a feeling of fear, dread and uneasiness4. Everyone feels it sometimes. In fact, some fears are expected at certain ages. Young children often have short-lived fears of the dark, storms, animals or strangers5. These usually fade as a child grows.

An anxiety disorder is different. The anxiety does not go away, it is felt in many situations, and it can get worse over time3. It can start to get in the way of schoolwork and relationships3.

Anxiety is one of the most common mental health conditions in childhood. Based on U.S. data from 2023 to 2024, about 11% of children ages 3 to 17 had a current anxiety diagnosis1. If your child is struggling, you are far from alone.

Anxiety can take different forms. The CDC describes several common types in children2:

  • Separation anxiety: being very afraid when away from parents or caregivers.
  • Specific phobia: extreme fear of one thing or situation, such as dogs, insects or going to the doctor.
  • Social anxiety: being very afraid of places with people, such as school.
  • Generalized anxiety: ongoing worry that is hard to control and gets in the way of daily activities.
  • Panic disorder: repeated, sudden waves of intense fear with a pounding heart, trouble breathing, dizziness, shaking or sweating.

Signs of anxiety in children to watch for

Anxiety does not always look like fear. Children often cannot put their worry into words, so it may show up in their bodies and behavior instead. Watch for:

  • Worry or fear that seems out of proportion to the situation
  • Constant worries about family, school, friends or activities5
  • Irritability, anger or meltdowns, especially before a feared event2
  • Trouble falling asleep or staying asleep2
  • Frequent stomachaches, headaches or tiredness with no clear medical cause2
  • Avoiding school, parties, sports or other everyday activities4
  • Needing a lot of reassurance, or asking "what if" questions over and over
  • Clinging to a parent or getting very upset at drop-off2

Some anxious children are quiet, obedient and eager to please, so their anxiety is easy to miss5. Others keep their worries to themselves2. A calm, well-behaved child can still be struggling on the inside.

Tip: Anxious children often complain of a stomachache on school mornings or before a test. After a doctor rules out a medical cause, notice when the pain shows up. A pattern can point to worry.

What the research says helps

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is the best-studied treatment for anxiety in children. CBT is a type of talk therapy. It teaches children to notice anxious thoughts, find more helpful ways of thinking, and slowly face their fears in small, manageable steps2. This gradual practice is called exposure.

A large Cochrane review looked at 87 studies with almost 6,000 children and teens7. After treatment, about 49% of young people who received CBT no longer had their main anxiety disorder, compared with about 18% of those on a waiting list7. The review found no clear evidence that one way of delivering CBT works better than another, such as group versus individual sessions, or sessions that include parents7. It also found little evidence that CBT works better than usual care or other active treatments, so it is not the only option7.

The American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry (AACAP) recommends that CBT be offered to children and teens ages 6 to 18 with common anxiety disorders6. It also says that certain medicines, called SSRIs, have considerable research support as safe and effective short-term treatments6. For some children, especially when anxiety is severe, combining CBT with medicine may work better in the short term than either one alone, although the research on this is still limited6. A health care provider can help you decide whether medicine should be part of the plan2.

Parents are part of the solution

Research suggests that changing how parents respond to anxiety can help a lot. Loving parents can easily fall into what experts call family accommodation. That means changing family routines to help a child avoid what scares them. Examples include sleeping with a child who has separation anxiety, or not inviting guests over because a child has social anxiety8. Accommodation can calm a child in the moment, but over time it can keep anxiety going8.

One study tested a program for parents only, called SPACE (Supportive Parenting for Anxious Childhood Emotions)8. In SPACE, parents learn to reduce accommodation and respond with support instead. The study included 124 children ages 7 to 148. The parent-only program worked as well as CBT delivered directly to the child, and parents in both groups reported less parenting stress8.

AACAP also notes that family-based work can support a child's treatment by reducing parents' own anxiety and teaching parenting skills that ease anxiety6. For very young children, involving parents in treatment is especially important2.

What you can do at home

These steps will not replace professional care, but they can make a real difference day to day.

  1. Listen and name the feeling. Say something like, "You feel scared about the sleepover. That makes sense." Naming the feeling helps a child feel understood.
  2. Show confidence in your child. Pair support with belief: "This is hard, and I know you can handle it." This tells your child you understand the fear and believe they can cope.
  3. Notice where you accommodate. Write down the ways your family works around the anxiety, such as answering the same worried question again and again, or letting your child skip activities. Pick one small change to start8.
  4. Encourage small brave steps. Instead of avoiding a fear completely, break it into tiny steps. A child afraid of dogs might first look at pictures, then watch a dog from across the street. Facing fears gradually is a core part of treatment2.
  5. Praise effort, not just results. Notice when your child tries something hard, even if it does not go perfectly.
  6. Take care of your own stress. Children learn by watching how adults handle worry. Getting support for yourself also helps your child6.

When to get help

Talk with your child's pediatrician or a mental health professional if:

  • Worries or fears are strong and last more than a few weeks
  • Anxiety is getting in the way of school, sleep, friendships or family life5
  • Your child avoids school or activities they used to enjoy
  • Physical complaints keep coming back and a doctor has found no medical cause
  • Your family is changing more and more routines to work around the fear8
  • You simply feel unsure, worried or stuck

The first step is a careful evaluation, so the right diagnosis can guide the right treatment2. The U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommends anxiety screening for children ages 8 to 18, so you can ask about it at your child's next checkup2.

If your child ever talks about wanting to die or hurting themselves, call or text 988 right away for free help, 24 hours a day2.

Frequently asked questions

What are the first signs of anxiety in a child?

Common early signs include worry that does not go away, frequent stomachaches or headaches, trouble sleeping, irritability and avoiding places like school. Some anxious children are quiet and eager to please, so their anxiety can be easy to miss.

Is it normal for my child to have fears?

Yes. Short-lived fears of the dark, storms, animals or strangers are expected at certain ages. It becomes a concern when the fear does not go away, shows up in many situations and gets in the way of school, sleep, friendships or family life.

What is the best treatment for anxiety in children?

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is the best-studied treatment, and the American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry recommends it for children and teens ages 6 to 18. Some children also benefit from medicine. A health care provider who knows your child can help decide what fits.

How can parents help a child with anxiety?

Listen, name the feeling and show confidence that your child can cope. Try to reduce family accommodation, the ways routines change to help a child avoid fears, and encourage small brave steps instead. In one study, a parent-only program worked as well as CBT delivered to the child.

When should I get help for my child's anxiety?

Reach out if worries are strong, last more than a few weeks, or get in the way of school, sleep, friendships or family life. Your child's pediatrician is a good first stop, and anxiety screening is recommended for children ages 8 to 18.
